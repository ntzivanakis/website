---
title: "What should AI be predicting?"
date: 2026-10-18
---

Every AI system used in public life is built to predict something, and it helps to be clear about what that means. An AI system is a statistical model. It learns patterns from past data and uses them to make a guess. The chatbots many of us now use are guessing the next word in a sentence, one word at a time. A council's risk model guesses which households are likely to fall behind on rent, based on households that did so before. Neither understands the people it is guessing about. Their outputs are informed guesses, and they should be treated as exactly that.

That is why I see AI as a tool that helps people make better-informed decisions. The decision itself belongs to a person, someone who can weigh the guess against what they know and who answers for the result.

What a system predicts is also a human choice. Someone makes it, usually early in a project and often without much discussion, and that choice decides whom the system serves. When I think about what AI and data for social purpose should mean, this is where I start.

Economists have met this problem before. For decades, countries measured their progress by GDP, the value of everything they produce, and governments steered by it. Economists such as Tony Atkinson, Amartya Sen, and Joseph Stiglitz showed that GDP is a poor guide to whether people's lives are going well. A measure of progress has to follow what people value. AI brings the same problem back, faster. A model applies its target to every case it sees, without tiring, so a badly chosen target can harm many people quickly.

Take two models built from the same council records. One predicts which households will cost the council most next year. The other predicts which households will fall behind on rent in the next three months, while there is still time to offer help. Both can be accurate, and a council has a use for each. Only the second is built around what the household needs. The data and the methods are the same. The question is different.

So what would AI and data for social purpose mean in practice? I see social purpose as a test that any system can pass or fail, whether a charity or a bank runs it. I would apply the test at four points.

First, what the system predicts. That should be decided with the people it affects, in terms they would recognise as a better life, and written down where others can check it.

Second, who is in the data. A model learns only from the people it has records for, so people who barely appear in the data get poor guesses. These are often the people public services most need to see: those in cash-in-hand or unrecorded work, and those living in small areas that surveys barely reach. Much of my own work, estimating conditions in small neighbourhoods and measuring the hidden economy, is about getting these people into the numbers before anyone builds on them.

Third, how often the system is wrong, and for whom. Every model gets things wrong some of the time, and rarely at the same rate for everyone. A system with a social purpose reports its mistakes group by group. It also says what happens to a person it gets wrong, and how that person can challenge the result.

Fourth, who decides. A model's output is one piece of evidence for a person to weigh, and it should never be the decision itself. Councils and ministries need staff of their own who can say what a model should predict and check whether it does. Those staff also need the authority to set its answer aside. A public body that cannot question a model does not control it.

Caution does not mean standing still. Used carefully, AI can put evidence within reach of people who never had it. Language models can already help sort and summarise research studies for evidence reviews, work that takes many weeks by hand. They can also be confidently wrong, so a researcher checks what they produce. The same tools can help a small council with no analysts of its own, or a local charity, find the evidence behind a decision and see where it comes from. In each case the tool does the searching and sorting, and a person does the judging.

Getting there is shared work. People who build models can publish what their models predict and where they go wrong, as routinely as they publish how often they are right. Funders and commissioners can pay for results people value, and refuse to buy systems they cannot inspect. Public bodies can treat in-house data expertise as essential, the way they treat legal advice. And anyone, as a resident or a claimant, can ask two questions of a system used in decisions about them: what is it predicting, and who checked?

AI will serve everyone well when the people it affects have a say in what it predicts. The final call should rest with a person who can question its output. That effort begins with measurement, which is where much of my own research starts. If you work on these questions, in public services or in industry, I would like to hear from you.

---
title: "What should AI be predicting?"
date: 2026-10-18
---

Every AI system used in public life is built to predict something. A model may estimate which households will fall behind on rent, or which benefit claim looks unusual. People choose that target, usually early in a project and often without much discussion, and the choice decides whom the system serves. It is where I start when I think about what AI and data for social purpose should mean.

Economists have met this problem before. For decades, national progress was measured by GDP and governments steered by it. The welfare measurement tradition I work in, built by Atkinson, Sen, and Stiglitz among others, showed that output is a poor guide to whether lives are going well. A measure of progress has to follow what people value. AI brings the same problem back at a different speed. A model pursues its target with a consistency no ministry can match, so a badly chosen target does its damage quickly and to many people at once.

Take two models built from the same council records. One predicts which households will cost the council most next year. The other predicts which households will fall into rent arrears in the next three months, while there is still time to offer advice. Both can be accurate, and a council has a use for each. Only the second is built around what the household needs. The data and the methods are identical; what differs is the question.

So what would AI and data for social purpose entail? I read social purpose as a test that any system can pass or fail, whether it runs in a charity or a bank. I would apply it in four places.

Start with the target. The outcome a system predicts should be defined with the people it affects, in terms they would recognise as a better life. It should also be written down where others can check it.

Then look at who is in the data. People who are thin in the data get thin predictions. Public services most need to see informal workers, and people in small areas that surveys barely reach. These are also the groups a model is most likely to miss. Much of my own work on small-area estimation and the informal economy is about making these people visible in the numbers before anyone builds on them.

Every model is wrong some of the time, and rarely at the same rate for everyone. A system with a social purpose reports its errors group by group and says what happens to a person it gets wrong.

Last comes control. A public body that cannot question a model does not control it. Councils and ministries need staff of their own who can say what a model should predict, and check whether it does.

None of this makes caution the default. Used well, AI widens who can use evidence. Language models can already screen and code studies for systematic reviews, work that takes many weeks by hand. The same tools can serve organisations that could never pay for a research team, such as a small council with no analysts of its own or a local charity. They can find the evidence behind a decision and see where it comes from.

Getting there is shared work. Researchers and developers can publish what their models target and how they fail, as routinely as they publish accuracy. Funders and commissioners can pay for outcomes people value, and decline to buy systems they cannot inspect. Public bodies can treat data skills as core staff, the way they treat legal advice. And anyone, as a resident or a claimant, can ask two questions of a system that makes decisions about them: what is it predicting, and who checked?

AI will be useful for everyone when the people it serves have a say in what it is trying to achieve, and the means to see whether it does. That effort begins with measurement, which is where much of my own research starts. If you work on these questions, in public services or in industry, I would like to hear from you.
